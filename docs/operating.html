LubbDubb · operator's guide

You stop producing the change. You start owning the verdicts.

Using an agent is a tool. LubbDubb is a harness: it watches your tracker, your pull requests and your CI on a heartbeat, decides what to do next, and puts agents on it — then hands you back the handful of decisions that were actually yours all along.

01 — The shift

What moves off your desk, and what stays on it

Three rows never move. They are the rows that need judgement, and judgement is the one thing the harness will not fake for you.

You used to Now
Pick the next ticket The dispatcher ranks every candidate; you re-order Up next if it's wrong
Cut the branch, open the PR An agent does, one per part, each in its own worktree
Chase red CI Each failing check is classified and worked — or held, if it isn't ours to fix
Write the change An agent writes it. You read the plan, then the delivered result
Decide whether the goal is even clear Still you yours
Decide what "done" means Still you yours
Decide whether this may leave the building Still you yours

02 — The unit of work

Stop thinking in branches. Think in goals.

The harness cuts branches and reaps them. A goal is a ticket, and every goal runs the same path — whether you wrote the ticket yourself or typed a sentence into Launch, which files one for you and sends it through the same funnel.

inTicket
gateEnough information?
thenPlan the work
gate · yoursPlan accepted?
thenDo the work
gate · yoursGoal achieved?
outReport

The gates are decisions, not formalities. Each one is something that has to be decided, not a step that always passes — and "goal achieved: no" returns to planning, never straight back to coding, because what's missing may be a different decomposition.

03 — The job

Everything you do in a day is one of five moves

1

State a goal well enough to be worked

Write for a reader who has the code, but not your last three weeks.

  • Say what should be true when it's done, not what to type.
  • Name the thing — file, route, screen, symptom. An agent can search; it can't guess which of four panels you meant.
  • Paste the evidence. Attachments follow the issue through to the agent working it; images paste straight into the composer.
  • Don't write the diff. A ticket that dictates the implementation gets you a worse one, and hides that you hadn't decided the outcome.
2

Answer what's in "Needs you"

The left rail is your whole inbox. Red first — those are costing you a slot.

  • Red means an agent is parked on a question only you can answer, holding a fleet slot and a worktree while it waits.
  • Amber means the obligation is yours and nothing in the fleet is blocked.
  • Answer on the goal's page, not from the row. An escalation read without its goal is a sentence with no subject.
3

Steer mid-run, sparingly

The drawer is for "you're going the wrong way" — not for coding by proxy.

  • Click any agent for its live transcript, its reasoning, and a box to type into.
  • If you're steering every agent one instruction at a time, the ticket was underspecified. Kill the run and fix the goal.
  • This is the most common way people fail here: it converts a fleet back into one pair of hands.
4

Judge what was delivered

Asked of what actually landed — never of the agent's confidence in itself.

  • Mark done when the goal is met.
  • Work left when it isn't — a third control, not the toggle's other end, because it's what puts the goal back in front of the harness.
  • Raise a bug when you ran it and it doesn't do what you expect. That's the one fact no agent on the goal can derive: none of them ran it.
5

Conclude, or let it end

A fleet whose goals never conclude is a rail nobody reads any more.

  • The harness never closes your ticket. A delivered goal whose item is still open files a close-out on your bench.
  • That one settles itself the moment the tracker stops listing the item open.
  • Everything else ends by Mark done, Work left, or End the run.

04 — The rail

Six things the harness will ask you for

Ask What it is You
blocking Escalation A parked agent asking you something Type the answer; it unparks
blocking Plan proposal A decomposition into parts, before any of it is built Accept, or reject with a reason
blocking Outbound act Something an agent wants to send into the world Accept or reject
blocking Permission An agent hit a command outside the allow-list Allow or refuse
yours Bench task Work only a person can do — flip a setting, look at a screen Do it, then mark done
yours Close-out A delivered goal whose ticket is still open Close it — it settles itself

A rejection isn't a dead end, and isn't a mute button. It stands until the world gives a reason to ask again — a push, a CI result, an approval, a comment — and the reason you typed is handed to the next agent that works that item. Write it as if the next agent will read it, because it will.

The plan proposal is the highest-leverage thirty seconds in your day. It's the last cheap moment to change the shape of the work. Read the parts and their dependency chain, not the prose: is this the right decomposition, is anything missing, is one part doing two things. Rejecting costs one replan. Accepting a bad shape costs you every PR underneath it.

05 — Rhythm

A day, concretely

  1. First
    Clear the recovery banner, if it's up A previous run left agents orphaned. While it stands no cycle runs at all, so everything else on screen is stale for one reason. Restore, requeue or remove each one.
  2. Then
    Drain the rail, red first Red rows hold agents that cost you a slot and a worktree while they wait. The rail sorts most-holding first for exactly that reason, and never re-sorts under you.
  3. Glance
    Four readings in the top bar Faults, then Output, then Yield, then Spend — in that order. What each is for is below.
  4. Check
    Up next The dispatcher's ranked plan, cut at your current headroom, every row carrying its reason verbatim. Wrong order? Drag it; the override persists. Wrong reasons? That's a configuration conversation, not a drag.
  5. Periodically
    Triage the backlog Nothing in it blocks an agent — which is why it's a tab and not a rail. The unwatched count in the nav is the one number that says whether opening it is worth it.
  6. When non-zero
    Read findings Claims agents filed about things outside their own task. They queue nothing by design — one agent's hunch spending another agent's slot is a capability escalation, not a convenience. Promote it, file it for later, or dismiss it.

06 — Instruments

Four readings, and the one number that matters

Faults

Recorded failures. Blocks nothing.

A rising count means something is quietly not working.

Output

The only reading against time — the only way to tell a fleet that's producing from one that's merely busy.

Dispatches per merge: effort over output.

Yield

How runs ended, the CI red rate, and the median time back to green.

The repeats table — a goal that quietly went round four times.

Spend

Where the money went, split by phase and by goal.

Which phase is eating the budget, not the total.

A rising dispatch line over a flat merge line is a fleet spinning, and the fix is almost always upstream of the agents: goals too vague, plans too coarse, or a check nobody classified so an agent keeps being sent at a wall it was never getting through.

Throughput has two dials, and neither is the model. Concurrency is the fleet cap, adjustable live — but its real ceiling is how fast you answer the rail, so if the rail is always full, lower it. Parked agents holding slots are worse than idle ones. The other dial is how much you had to answer: a goal that ran ticket to merge on two clicks is the point; a goal that took nine escalations was a goal that was never stated.

07 — Failure modes

Four habits that don't survive the move

  • ✕ Watching work happen. Following one transcript to the end feels like productivity and is the most expensive hour you can spend here. Open a drawer to steer or diagnose, then close it.
  • ✕ Doing it yourself because it's faster. Sometimes true, and fine — but do it as a ticket the harness knows about, or the record has a hole in it and the close-out sweep will ask you about work you already did.
  • ✕ Batching the rail. Asks are cheap to answer and expensive to leave. Each blocking one is an agent holding a slot. The rail is not email.
  • ✕ Treating a plan rejection as a failure. It's the mechanism working. A replan costs one agent; an accepted bad plan costs every PR under it.

08 — Fixed

Two things you can't configure away

Both exist so this is safe to leave running. Understand them before trying to work around them.

Every act that reaches the outside world is authorized. By you — either on the act itself, or by a standing landing you clicked over a named stack. There is no arm where an agent posts, merges or files unasked, and no setting that adds one.
An agent declares that it finished. Nothing infers it. Silence never reads as success. The failure this chooses is the cheap, visible one: work sitting still with a marker on it, rather than work quietly assumed complete.

09 — Ramp

Your first week

  1. Day 1
    Watch it turn Run the example config's mock agent, or a real repo with a small watched label set. Force a pulse with Scan and read the decision log: what was decided, and which rule produced it. Nothing here is unexplainable — including an idle cycle.
  2. Days 2–3
    One goal, end to end Pick a real ticket you'd have done yourself. Let it run. Answer everything. Then compare the result to what you'd have written — and note where the ticket, not the agent, was the limiting factor.
  3. Day 4
    Turn the cap up Two or three agents. This is where operating starts feeling different from using: you'll notice you're now reading rather than typing.
  4. Day 5
    Tune what you saw The CI policy, so a check nobody here can fix holds instead of sending an agent at a wall. The prompt book, if the house style of your reports or tickets is wrong. The watch labels, if the wrong things are being picked up.
